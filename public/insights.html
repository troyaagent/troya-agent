Robinhood Chain in five minutes

Chain · 10 Nov 2026

Robinhood Chain is an Ethereum L2 built on Arbitrum Orbit (Nitro), with chain ID 4663. Like any rollup it batches transactions cheaply and settles them to Ethereum. Gas is paid in ETH, so the same coin you already hold on Ethereum, Arbitrum or Base pays for every swap and launch.

Because it is an EVM chain, ordinary wallets work once the network is added: chain ID 4663, currency ETH, and a public RPC. The Troya app can add it for you from the Bridge page, and every transaction can be looked up on robinhoodchain.blockscout.com.

Getting ETH onto Robinhood Chain is a bridge from Arbitrum, Base or Ethereum through a LI.FI route, and it usually lands in minutes. Going back out works the same way, through the same routes.

The chain carries 63 tokenized stocks and ETFs, such as NVDA, TSLA, AAPL, SPY and QQQ, which Troya lists with prices from their on-chain pools. It also carries a steady stream of brand-new coins. On Pons Family a coin trades on its bonding curve until it graduates, at 4.2 ETH raised on ETH-paired curves, into a Uniswap v4 pool with the Pons hook, and Clanker, Bankr, Virtuals and Hoodit launch coins here too. That stream of fresh tokens is exactly why Troya leans so heavily on reading before acting.

How Troya Gate reads a pool

Safety · 10 Nov 2026

Before a swap is shown to you, and again before the Agent Wallet would sign it, Troya Gate runs seven checks. Is the route made of a pool or curve that exists right now? How far does this order push the price? Is there enough ETH behind the quote to get back out? How much do the pool and launch fees take? Does the exact swap succeed in a simulation? Does a test sell straight after go through, or does it revert? Does the token lose part of a transfer to a hidden tax?

The checks are deliberately boring. They catch the most common ways a fresh token takes your ETH: a honeypot that cannot be sold, a pool too shallow to exit, a contract that quietly keeps a cut on every transfer.

When a check fails, Troya does not argue with you or quietly adjust the order. It stops, names the check that failed and shows the number behind it. What you do next is your decision.

Why the limits live outside the model

Design · 10 Nov 2026

It is tempting to tell a language model "never trade more than 100 dollars" and call it a limit. It is not one. A model can misread the instruction, be talked out of it, or be steered by text it reads along the way, including text planted in a token name.

Troya keeps limits in a policy file on the server. The model never sees an editable copy and cannot write to it. Every tool call that could spend money loads the policy fresh and checks the order against it before anything is signed. If the policy says no, the answer is no, however good the model's reasoning sounded.

The model's job is to read, explain and propose. The policy's job is to decide what is allowed. Keeping those two jobs apart is the whole point.

Agent Wallet: pocket money, not a vault

Wallets · 10 Nov 2026

Signing every trade yourself is the safest way to use Troya, and it is the default. The Agent Wallet exists for the moments when that is impractical: a stop-loss that should fire while you sleep, a DCA that runs every week.

It is custodial. The operator holds its key, encrypted at rest, so the agent can sign without asking you each time. That convenience is also the risk, which is why it only acts inside your caps, only withdraws to your own address, lets you export its key at any time and never touches your main wallet.

The rule of thumb is simple: keep in it only what you would be comfortable losing.

Prices in ETH, and why USD is an estimate

Data · 10 Nov 2026

Most new coins on Robinhood Chain trade against ETH, on an ETH-paired Pons curve or a WETH pool, so ETH is the honest unit for their prices. Some curves and pools pair with USDG instead. Troya shows the price in the pair token first and converts to USD at the current ETH price. The conversion is good enough to compare tokens, but it moves whenever ETH moves. Tokenized stocks are priced from their own on-chain pools, so read them as what the pool says right now.

Market caps on the board are estimates from total supply. For a token whose supply sits mostly in a locked pool, that number can look far larger than what anyone could actually sell into. Liquidity and 24h volume usually tell you more.

Dry-run first: connecting your agent safely

MCP · 10 Nov 2026

Add the Troya MCP server to Claude Desktop, Cursor or Claude Code with one command and you get the read tools straight away. No token is needed to look at markets.

If you add an API token for Agent Wallet tools, execution still starts in dry-run: your agent can prepare orders, see them pass or fail Troya Gate, and read what would have happened, without sending anything. Spend a day there. When the proposals look the way you expect, switch dry-run off for that token in the app.
